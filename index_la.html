Seeing linear maps

These simulators are meant to support the study of linear algebra.

Linear algebra is the working language of physics, engineering and data science. Its theorems are proved with symbols, yet each one describes something that happens in space. Take one idea at a time, move it with your own hands, and stay with it until the formula in your textbook and the picture on the screen say the same thing.

1A matrix moves the plane

A 2 × 2 matrix is four numbers. It is also a rule that moves every point of the plane, and the four numbers say where two particular arrows land. In the picture, gray shows the plane before the map and color shows it after. Drag the tips of the red and blue arrows and watch everything else follow.

Try this

  1. Start from nothing. Press Identity. The colored grid now lies exactly on the gray one, and A is the identity matrix. Drag the tip of the red arrow. Only the first column of A changes.
  2. Now the other arrow. Drag the tip of the blue arrow. Only the second column changes. You are writing the matrix by hand: its columns are the places where e1 and e2 land.
  3. Follow one vector. The gray arrow is v. It starts at (2, 1): two steps along e1, then one step along e2. Its image Av is reached by the same recipe on the new grid: two steps along the red arrow, then one along the blue. Drag v around. The thick red and blue path keeps to the recipe wherever you go.
  4. Watch a map happen. Press Shear, Rotate 30° and Stretch in turn. Each time, check three things: the origin stays where it is, straight lines stay straight, and evenly spaced lines stay evenly spaced.
  5. Build a quarter turn. Without calculating, drag the red arrow to where a 90° counterclockwise turn would carry e1, and the blue arrow to where it would carry e2. Then read the matrix. The caption under the grid tells you when you have it.
  6. Break it. Lay the blue arrow along the same line as the red one, and the plane collapses onto that line. Then carry the blue arrow across to the far side of the red one. The plane has been turned over, and the highlighted cell turns pink and hatched to show it.

About the animation

A linear map has no in-between. It gives each input one output, and that is all. The animation is there so your eye can follow each point to where it lands. It turns the plane steadily while scaling it along two perpendicular directions. Section 2 shows that turning and stretching are enough to build any matrix.

What you just saw

Two arrows decide everything. Once you know where e1 and e2 land, you know where every vector lands, because every vector is a combination of those two and a linear map carries combinations to combinations:

v = xe1 + ye2 so Av = x Ae1 + y Ae2

Write the two landing places as the columns of A, and that sentence turns into the rule for multiplying a matrix by a vector:

abcd xy = x ac + y bd = ax + bycx + dy

So the product Av is a recipe. Take x copies of the first column and y copies of the second, and add them. The formula on the far right is the same recipe written one coordinate at a time.

The idea to keep. A linear map is completely determined by what it does to a basis. A matrix is the record of that: column k is the image of the kth basis vector.

Reading with Axler

In Linear Algebra Done Right (4th edition) this section belongs with Chapter 3. Result 3.4 in Section 3A, the linear map lemma, says that a linear map is fixed by its values on a basis, and that those values can be chosen freely. Dragging the two arrows is that lemma in your hands. Definition 3.31 in Section 3C builds the matrix ℳ(T) one column at a time from the vectors Tvk. Here the space is R2 with the standard basis, so the matrix A can stand in for the map T, and Av means Tv.

What linear looks like

A map T is linear when it respects the two operations a vector space has, addition and scaling:

T(u + v) = Tu + Tv and T(λv) = λ Tv

In the simulator those two rules have visible consequences. The origin cannot move. Straight lines stay straight, parallel lines stay parallel, and evenly spaced lines stay evenly spaced. The only way a line can stop being a line is by collapsing to a point, as in step 6. A map that bends the grid or shifts the origin cannot be written as a matrix.

2Turn and stretch

A shear looks like neither a rotation nor a stretch. Yet it can be carried out in three plain moves: turn the plane, stretch it along the two axes, and turn it again. So can every other 2 × 2 matrix. Press Play to watch the three moves, then drag the arrows and look for a matrix that needs anything more.

Try this

  1. Play the shear. The section opens on a shear. Press Play. The plane turns, then stretches along the horizontal and vertical axes, then turns again, and it finishes as exactly the shear. Use the slider beside the button to go through the moves at your own pace, or press the numbers 1, 2 and 3 to play one move at a time.
  2. Watch the circle. The gray circle is the unit circle before the map. Turning leaves a circle alone and only the stretch changes its shape, so it ends as an ellipse. The two dark lines are the axes of that ellipse. The numbers at their ends are the two stretch factors.
  3. Follow the cross. The dashed gray cross marks two perpendicular directions on the circle. Play again and follow its ends along the dotted trails. The first turn lays the cross on the coordinate axes, the stretch slides its ends along those axes, and the second turn carries it to where the ellipse needs it. It stays perpendicular the whole way.
  4. Switch on the fitted grid. Tick Fitted grid. This is a square grid, tilted to line up with the gray cross. Play again. Its squares become rectangles and never slant. Every matrix has a grid like this.
  5. Look for an exception. Drag the red and blue arrows to any matrix you like, or try the presets. The three moves are always there. For Stretch both turns are zero. For Rotate 30° both stretch factors are 1. For Reflect one stretch factor is negative. For Flatten one is zero.
  6. Build a matrix from the moves. Now go the other way and move the four sliders. Two angles and two stretch factors are four numbers, the same count as the four entries of the matrix.

Several recipes, one matrix

The recipe is not unique. Adding a half turn to both turns changes nothing. A quarter turn can also move from one turn to the other if the two stretch factors swap places.

When you set a matrix by hand, the simulator shows the recipe with the smallest first turn, and with positive stretch factors whenever the map keeps the orientation of the plane.

What you just saw

Every 2 × 2 matrix can be written as a product of three simple ones: a rotation, a stretch along the axes, and a second rotation.

A = R(φ) s100s2 R(θ) where R(θ) = cos θ−sin θsin θcos θ

Read the product from right to left, because the matrix written nearest to a vector acts on it first. First R(θ) turns the plane by θ. Then the middle matrix stretches it by s1 along the horizontal axis and by s2 along the vertical one. Last, R(φ) turns it by φ.

A 2 × 2 matrix has four entries. The three moves also take four numbers: two angles and two stretch factors. Each setting of the four sliders gives a matrix, and each matrix has such a recipe.

The idea to keep. Every linear map of the plane sends some pair of perpendicular directions to a pair of perpendicular directions, stretching each by its own factor. The dashed gray cross and the dark cross are those two pairs.

Reading with Axler

This is the singular value decomposition: result 7.70 in Section 7E of Linear Algebra Done Right (4th edition). Axler’s singular values are the sizes of the two stretch factors, listed from larger to smaller.

His version does not ask the two outer moves to be turns. Any maps that preserve lengths will do, reflections included, and that is why his singular values are never negative. Here both outer moves are kept as true turns, so a flip shows up as a negative stretch factor instead.

Result 7.99 in Section 7F is the picture on this page: an invertible operator takes the unit ball to an ellipsoid.

Why the circle becomes an ellipse

A turn carries the unit circle onto itself. Only the stretch changes its shape, and a circle stretched along two perpendicular axes is an ellipse. So the half-lengths of the axes of the ellipse are the sizes of the two stretch factors, whatever the matrix looked like at first.

The special cases follow from the same picture. If the two stretch factors are equal, the circle stays a circle and the matrix is a turn combined with a uniform scaling. If one stretch factor is zero, the ellipse is flattened to a line segment and the map cannot be undone. If one is negative, the ellipse looks the same as it would for the positive factor, but the plane has been turned over.

3The determinant as area

The determinant of a 2 × 2 matrix is one number, ad − bc. It says what the map does to area: by what factor every region grows or shrinks, and whether the plane has been turned over. Drag the arrows and watch the highlighted region and the number change together.

Try this

  1. Read the area. The highlighted parallelogram is what the unit square becomes, and its area is printed inside it. The number det A in the panel is the same. Drag the arrow tips and watch the two stay equal.
  2. Change the shape, keep the area. Press Shear, Rotate 30° and Stretch. The three maps look nothing alike, yet each has determinant 1. They rearrange the plane without changing any area.
  3. Turn the plane over. Carry the blue arrow across the red one. The area shrinks to zero and grows again, but now the determinant is negative, and the small curved arrow from red to blue runs clockwise. The sign records that the plane has been turned over.
  4. Flatten it. Lay the two arrows along one line, or press Flatten. The parallelogram has no area, and the determinant is zero.
  5. Try other regions. Choose Triangle, F and Disc. Each has its own area before the map, and each is scaled by the same factor. The F also shows a negative determinant at a glance: it comes out as its mirror image.
  6. Slide it into a rectangle. Go back to Square and press Slide. First the blue tip slides parallel to the red arrow until the blue arrow stands on an axis. Then the red tip slides parallel to the blue arrow. Neither slide changes the base or the height, so the area printed in the picture stays the same. What is left is a rectangle, and its sides can be read off.

Area with a sign

An area is never negative. The determinant is an area with a sign attached. It is positive when the short way from the red arrow to the blue arrow is counterclockwise, as it is from e1 to e2, and negative when it is clockwise.

What you just saw

Take the matrix with columns (a, c) and (b, d). The first slide carries the blue tip from (b, d) to the point (0, d − bc/a) on the vertical axis. The second carries the red tip from (a, c) to (a, 0). The rectangle that is left is a wide and d − bc/a high, so its area is

a (d − bc/a) = ad − bc

That is the formula for the determinant of a 2 × 2 matrix. The sign comes out right as well, because sliding never turns the plane over. When c is larger in size than a, the simulator slides the arrows onto the other two axes instead. The area is the same either way.

det abcd = ad − bc

The idea to keep. The determinant is the factor by which a linear map scales every area, with a minus sign when the map turns the plane over. It is zero exactly when the map flattens the plane.

Reading with Axler

Axler leaves determinants to the end of the book. Chapter 9 of Linear Algebra Done Right (4th edition) defines them through alternating multilinear forms, and no picture of area appears in the definition.

The geometry comes earlier, in Section 7F, where operators are applied to ellipsoids and parallelepipeds. A parallelepiped in the plane is the parallelogram on this page.

Why every region scales by the same factor

A linear map sends every small square of the grid to a copy of the same small parallelogram. A region built from such squares is therefore scaled by the same factor as each square, and any reasonable region can be filled with small squares as closely as you like. That is why one number is enough.

Two consequences follow at once. Doing one map after another multiplies the two factors, so det(AB) = det A · det B. And in the language of section 2, turning changes no area while the stretch multiplies it by s1s2, so the determinant is the product of the two stretch factors.

4The determinant as volume

Three arrows in space span a slanted box. Its volume is the determinant of the 3 × 3 matrix whose columns are the three arrows, with a minus sign if the arrows are in left-hand order. Drag the picture to turn it, and drag the arrow tips to change the box.

Try this

  1. Look around. Drag anywhere in the picture to turn it, use the Turn and Tilt sliders, or tick Spin. The gray grid is the floor, the plane of e1 and e2. A dotted line drops from each raised arrow tip to the floor, so you can read off where the tip is.
  2. Base times height. The box stands on the parallelogram spanned by the red and blue arrows. The dashed line is the height of the green tip above that base. The panel multiplies the area of the base by the height, and the result is det A.
  3. Slide the top. Press Straighten. The top of the box slides parallel to its base until the box stands straight. The base and the height do not change, so the volume printed in the box does not change either.
  4. Same volume, other shape. Press Cube and then Brick. Both have determinant 1.
  5. Flatten it. Press Flat, or drag the green tip down into the plane of the other two arrows. The box has no volume and the determinant is zero.
  6. Change the order. Press Slanted and then Left-handed. The box is the same box, but the red and blue arrows have changed places. The determinant has changed sign, and the box turns pink. Mirror does it another way: it reflects the green arrow through the floor.

Right hand, left hand

Curl the fingers of your right hand from the red arrow toward the blue one, the way the small curved arrow runs. Your thumb then points along the thin dark arrow at the origin.

If the green arrow lies on the thumb’s side of the base, the three arrows are in right-hand order and the determinant is positive. If it lies on the other side, the order is left-handed and the determinant is negative.

What you just saw

The volume of a box is the area of its base times its height. Physics has a compact way to write that. The cross product Ae1 × Ae2 is a vector at right angles to the base, and its length is the area of the base. Its dot product with Ae3 multiplies that area by the height of Ae3 above the base, counted as negative when Ae3 is on the far side:

det A = (Ae1 × Ae2) · Ae3

Written out in the nine entries of the matrix, the same number is the formula you may have learned by heart:

det abcdefghi = a(ei − fh) − b(di − fg) + c(dh − eg)

The idea to keep. The determinant of a 3 × 3 matrix is the volume of the box spanned by its columns, with a minus sign when the columns are in left-hand order. It is zero exactly when the three columns lie in one plane.

Reading with Axler

The slanted box has a name, parallelepiped. Section 7F of Linear Algebra Done Right (4th edition) applies operators to ellipsoids and parallelepipeds.

The determinant itself is defined in Chapter 9 through alternating multilinear forms. An alternating form changes sign when two of its vectors change places, which is what the Left-handed preset shows.

The same idea in every dimension

Nothing in this picture is special to three dimensions. For an n × n matrix the determinant is the n-dimensional volume of the box spanned by the columns, with a sign for their order. It is zero exactly when the columns fail to span the whole space, which is the same as saying that the matrix cannot be inverted. Section 3 was the case n = 2, where volume is called area.

5One map after another

Do one linear map and then another, and the result is again a linear map. Its matrix is the product of the two matrices, written in the opposite order: first A and then B is the product BA. Drag the thin arrows to change A, and watch what B does with them.

Try this

  1. Read the picture. The opening A is a shear and B is a quarter turn. The thin arrows are Ae1 and Ae2, where A puts the basis vectors. The bold arrows are BAe1 and BAe2, where they end up after both maps. The dotted lines show B carrying each thin arrow to its bold one.
  2. Watch it in two steps. Press Play. The plane first moves by A, pauses, and then moves by B. The slider lets you stop anywhere on the way.
  3. Swap the order. Press Swap A and B. Now the turn comes first and the shear second, and the plane ends up somewhere else. The panel shows the same thing in numbers: AB is not BA.
  4. Columns again. Drag the tip of a thin arrow. Its bold arrow follows, because BAe1 is always B applied to Ae1. This is how the columns of a product are made.
  5. Try the presets. Two turns, by 30° and 60°, make a turn by 90° in either order. Two mirrors make a quarter turn, and in the other order the opposite quarter turn. Two Stretches along the same axes commute.
  6. Undo and flatten. Under Undo, B brings every vector back where it started: BA is the identity. Under Flatten, A crushes the plane onto a line, and no B can bring it back.

Reading with Axler

Axler defines the product of linear maps in Section 3A of Linear Algebra Done Right (4th edition), under Algebraic Operations on ℒ(V, W). Section 3C connects it with matrix multiplication, and Section 3D treats invertible maps.

What you just saw

Definition: product of linear maps

Suppose T and S are linear maps from the plane to itself. The product ST is the map defined by (ST)v = S(Tv). In words: first T, then S.

The product is again linear, and its matrix is the product of the two matrices in the same order. The order looks reversed because the vector is written on the right: in BAv = B(Av), the matrix next to v acts first.

One product, computed three ways

Take the opening pair: the shear A and the quarter turn B.

A = 1101 and B = 0−110

Row times column

BA = 0·1 + (−1)·00·1 + (−1)·11·1 + 0·01·1 + 0·1 = 0−111

Column by column, the way section 1 reads a matrix

B(Ae1) = B 10 = 1 01 + 0 −10 = 01

B(Ae2) = B 11 = 1 01 + 1 −10 = −11

As rules for coordinates, the way Axler writes maps

T(x, y) = (x + y, y), S(x, y) = (−y, x), so (ST)(x, y) = S(x + y, y) = (−y, x + y)

The first coordinate of the result is 0x − 1y and the second is 1x + 1y: these are the rows (0, −1) and (1, 1) of BA. The three ways agree, and the second is the one the simulator draws: each bold arrow is B applied to a thin one.

Why the order looks backwards

Functions work the same way: (f ∘ g)(x) = f(g(x)) means first g, then f. A matrix is a function of the vector, so the map that acts first stands next to the vector, on the right.

The other order

Now let the quarter turn act first.

AB = 1101 0−110 = 1−110

With coordinates, (TS)(x, y) = T(−y, x) = (x − y, x). So AB ≠ BA: turning and then shearing is not the same as shearing and then turning. Matrix multiplication is not commutative, and the simulator shows why. The two orders leave the plane in different places.

Some pairs do commute: two rotations of the plane, two stretches along the same axes, and any matrix with the identity. They are the exception, not the rule.

Areas multiply

Section 3 measured a map by the factor by which it scales area. If A scales every area by det A and B then scales every area by det B, the two together scale it by the product:

det(BA) = det B · det A

The preset Stretches shows it with the simplest numbers. A doubles the width, so it doubles every area, and B triples the height, so it triples every area:

1003 2001 = 2003 and 6 = 3 · 2

The panel works out the same product for any pair. One consequence: if either map flattens the plane, so does the product, because one of the two factors is 0.

Undoing a map

If BA = I, then B undoes A. Such a B is called the inverse of A and written A−1. A 2 × 2 matrix has an inverse exactly when its determinant is not 0, and then

abcd−1 = 1/(ad − bc) d−b−ca

Under Undo, A has the rows (2, 1) and (1, 1), and its determinant is 2 · 1 − 1 · 1 = 1. The formula gives the B of the preset, and the check is a product:

A−1A = 1−1−12 2111 = 2 − 11 − 1−2 + 2−1 + 2 = 1001

To undo two maps, undo them in the opposite order: (BA)−1 = A−1B−1. You take off your shoes before your socks.

The idea to keep. Doing A and then B is one linear map, BA. The second map is written on the left, and the columns of BA are B applied to the columns of A.

A product of turns

Turns by the angles α and β make a turn by α + β. Multiply the two rotation matrices out and you get the addition formulas of trigonometry, cos(α + β) and sin(α + β), for free.

Gates one after another

Section 11 applies gates to a qubit one after another, and the same rule holds. A quantum circuit is drawn from left to right, in the order in which the gates act, but the matrices are multiplied from right to left, as here. Apply H and then Z to |0⟩, and compare with the other order.

With kets

ZH|0⟩ = Z|+⟩ = |−⟩, but HZ|0⟩ = H|0⟩ = |+⟩

With columns

ZH 10 = 100−1 1/√2 11 = 1/√2 1−1

Here H acts first, so it stands next to the vector. The two orders give different states, and on the sphere of section 11 they sit at opposite ends of the x axis.

6Null space and range

A map that flattens the plane loses information. Its null space is everything that it sends to the origin, and its range is everything that it can reach. Drag v onto the dashed line and watch Av vanish.

Try this

  1. Read the picture. The opening matrix flattens the plane: its two columns lie on one line. The dashed line is the null space, the inputs that A sends to 0. The yellow line is the range, the outputs that A can reach.
  2. Watch the crush. Press Play. The dots on the dashed line slide together into the origin, while the whole plane folds down onto the yellow line.
  3. Find the null space. Drag v onto the dashed line, for example to (2, −1). Now Av = 0. Any multiple of this v works too.
  4. Many inputs, one output. Put v back at (1, 1) and drag it along the dotted line through it. Av does not move. The dotted line runs parallel to the null space, and adding a vector of the null space to v changes nothing in the output.
  5. What cannot be reached. No output ever leaves the yellow line. With the opening matrix, no v at all lands on (1, 0), however you drag it.
  6. Compare the presets. Identity and Shear crush nothing and reach everything. Project crushes the vertical axis and reaches the horizontal one. Under Same line, the null space and the range are the same line. Zero crushes everything.

Reading with Axler

This is Section 3B of Linear Algebra Done Right (4th edition), Null Spaces and Ranges. Its three parts are Null Space and Injectivity, Range and Surjectivity, and the Fundamental Theorem of Linear Maps.

What you just saw

Definition: null space, range

Suppose T is a linear map from V to W. The null space of T is the set of vectors that T sends to 0, and the range of T is the set of all its outputs:

null T = {v ∈ V : Tv = 0},  range T = {Tv : v ∈ V}.

Both are subspaces: lines through the origin in the picture, or the origin alone, or the whole plane. The null space lives among the inputs and the range among the outputs. Here the inputs and the outputs are the same plane, so both can be drawn on one sheet, and they can even be the same line.

One matrix, three ways

Take the opening matrix and find both subspaces.

A = 120.51

As equations

x + 2y = 0, 0.5x + y = 0

The second equation is half the first, so there is only one condition, x = −2y. The solutions are the multiples of (2, −1): the null space is a line.

With columns, the way section 1 reads a matrix

Av = x 10.5 + y 21 = (x + 2y) 10.5

The second column is twice the first, so every output is a multiple of (1, 0.5). That is the range. The output is 0 exactly when x + 2y = 0, which is the null space again.

As a rule for coordinates, the way Axler writes a map

T(x, y) = (x + 2y,  ½x + y) = (x + 2y) (1, ½)

All three give null T, the line through (2, −1), and range T, the line through (1, ½), which is also the line through (2, 1) that the panel names.

Two names

Many books call the null space the kernel, written ker T, and the range the image, written im T. Axler uses null and range.

The dimensions add up

Every matrix in the presets shares out the two dimensions of the plane between its null space and its range.

Presetdim nulldim rangesum
Identity, Shear022
Flatten, Project, Same line112
Zero202

This is no accident. For a linear map T from a finite-dimensional space V to any vector space, Axler proves

dim V = dim null T + dim range T.

Every dimension that the map crushes is a dimension that its range lacks. In the plane the only possible splits are 0 + 2, 1 + 1 and 2 + 0.

Crushing nothing means reaching everything

A map is injective if different inputs always give different outputs. If Tu = Tv, then T(u − v) = 0. So if the null space is {0}, then u = v. Conversely, an injective map sends only 0 to 0. Hence T is injective exactly when its null space is {0}. It is surjective if every output is reached, that is, if its range is everything.

For a map from the plane to itself, the theorem ties the two together. Null space {0} means dimension 0, so the range has dimension 2 and is the whole plane. Then the map can be undone, as in section 5, and its determinant from section 3 is not 0. A flat map fails all of these at once.

Solving equations

The equation Av = w has a solution exactly when w lies in the range. With the opening matrix, w = (3, 1.5) is in the range and v = (1, 1) solves it:

120.51 11 = 31.5

It is not the only solution. Adding any vector of the null space changes nothing, so all the solutions are

v = 11 + s 2−1 for every number s

This is the dotted line of the picture: a copy of the null space, moved aside to pass through one solution. The equation Av = (1, 0), on the other hand, has no solution at all, because the second coordinate of every output is half the first.

The idea to keep. The null space is what a map forgets, and the range is what it can still produce. In the plane their dimensions always add up to 2.

One solution or infinitely many

A linear system that has a solution has either one or infinitely many. The null space decides which: if it is {0}, the solution is unique.

A measurement forgets

The preset Project has a meaning in quantum mechanics. The matrix that keeps the |0⟩ part of a qubit state and drops the |1⟩ part is a projection. Physicists write it as a column times a row, |0⟩⟨0|:

|0⟩⟨0| = 10 10 = 1000

With kets

|0⟩⟨0| (α|0⟩ + β|1⟩) = α|0⟩

With columns

1000 αβ = α0

Its range is the line of |0⟩ and its null space is the line of |1⟩: the projection forgets β entirely. The squared length of what is left, |α|2, is the probability that a measurement gives 0, the number shown by the bar in section 11.

7Vectors that keep their direction

A matrix turns most vectors as it moves them. A few it does not turn: they stay on their own line and are only stretched. These are the eigenvectors of the matrix, and the stretch factors are its eigenvalues. Drag v until Av falls on the line of v.

Try this

  1. Read the picture. The gray arrow is v and the dark arrow is Av. The gray circle holds every vector as long as v, and the dark oval shows where the matrix sends them. The small arc at the origin shows how far v is turned.
  2. Find a vector that is not turned. Drag v around until Av lies on the dashed line through v. The line lights up. With the opening matrix this happens at v = (1, 1), where Av = (3, 3) = 3v.
  3. Find the other one. Go on to v = (1, −1). Here Av = v: the matrix leaves this vector as it is. The two eigenvalues of this matrix are 3 and 1.
  4. Let it sweep. Press Sweep. The vector goes once round its circle and rests for a moment on every eigenvector it meets. It rests four times, because v and −v lie on the same line.
  5. Try the presets. Shear has only one such line. Rotate 30° has none, because it turns every vector by 30°. Reflect sends one line to itself backwards: the eigenvalue is −1, and the line lights up in pink. Flatten has the eigenvalue 0: one line is crushed to the origin.
  6. Change the matrix. Drag the red and blue tips, and tick Show the eigen-lines to keep the lines in view as you go. Whenever the two numbers off the diagonal are equal, the two lines are perpendicular.

Reading with Axler

This is Section 5A of Linear Algebra Done Right (4th edition), Invariant Subspaces. Eigenvalues are defined in 5.5 and eigenvectors in 5.8.

Axler speaks of an operator T on a vector space V. Here T is multiplication by the matrix A, and V is the plane.

What you just saw

Definition: eigenvalue, eigenvector

Suppose A is a square matrix. A number λ is called an eigenvalue of A if there is a vector v ≠ 0 such that Av = λv. Such a vector v is called an eigenvector of A corresponding to λ.

In the picture, Av = λv says that the dark arrow lies on the line of the gray one. The number λ says how the length changes: more than 1 stretches, between 0 and 1 shrinks, 0 crushes, and a negative number flips the vector to the other side of the origin.

One calculation, written three ways

Take the opening matrix and v = (1, 1). The same calculation can be written in three ways, and books move between them freely.

As a matrix times a column

2112 11 = 2·1 + 1·11·1 + 2·1 = 33 = 3 11

As a sum of columns, the way section 1 reads a matrix

Av = 1 21 + 1 12 = 33

As a rule for coordinates, the way Axler writes an operator

T(x, y) = (2x + y,  x + 2y), so T(1, 1) = (3, 3) = 3 (1, 1)

All three lines say Av = 3v. So 3 is an eigenvalue of A, and (1, 1) is an eigenvector corresponding to it. In the same way A(1, −1) = (1, −1), so 1 is an eigenvalue with the eigenvector (1, −1).

Why the zero vector is left out

The zero vector satisfies A0 = λ0 for every number λ, so it singles out nothing. That is why the definition asks for v ≠ 0.

A whole line of them

If v is an eigenvector, so is every nonzero multiple of v, with the same eigenvalue. The matrix sends the line through v into itself. Axler calls such a line an invariant subspace of dimension one.

Finding eigenvalues by calculation

Move λv to the left side: Av = λv says (A − λI)v = 0. So λ is an eigenvalue exactly when the matrix A − λI sends some nonzero vector to zero. A matrix that does this flattens the plane, and section 3 gave the test for that: its determinant is zero.

det(A − λI) = (a − λ)(d − λ) − bc = 0

For the opening matrix this reads (2 − λ)2 − 1 = 0. So 2 − λ is 1 or −1, and λ is 1 or 3: the two numbers that the picture gave. To find the eigenvectors for 3, apply A − 3I to a general vector:

−111−1 xy = −x + yx − y

This is zero exactly when y = x. The eigenvectors for 3 are the nonzero vectors on the line through (1, 1), as the simulator showed.

Two lines, one line, or none

The equation for λ is quadratic, so a 2 × 2 matrix has at most two eigenvalues. The presets show every case.

Two eigenvalues. There are two eigen-lines. Eigenvectors that correspond to different eigenvalues never lie on one line, so one from each makes a basis of the plane. Measured in that basis the matrix is a stretch along the two lines and nothing else. A matrix with such a basis is called diagonalizable, and section 8 shows what it looks like in that basis.

One eigenvalue. The shear has a single eigen-line, and no basis of eigenvectors. A multiple of the identity is the other extreme: every nonzero vector is an eigenvector.

No real eigenvalue. A rotation turns every vector, so no real number λ works. The quadratic still has two solutions, but they are complex numbers. For a quarter turn they are i and −i.

When the matrix is symmetric, the two eigen-lines are perpendicular. Section 2 gave every matrix a pair of perpendicular stretch axes, but most matrices also turn those axes. A symmetric matrix does not: its stretch axes are its eigen-lines.

The idea to keep. An eigenvector is a direction that the matrix does not turn. Along that direction the whole matrix acts like a single number, the eigenvalue.

Axler does it without determinants

In 5.7 Axler lists conditions equivalent to being an eigenvalue, and in 5.19 he proves that an eigenvalue always exists over the complex numbers. No determinant is used. The formula on this page is a shortcut for small matrices.

The cases in the book

Eigenvectors for different eigenvalues are linearly independent (5.11), and there are never more eigenvalues than the dimension (5.12). Example 5.9 is the quarter turn with no real eigenvalue. Diagonalizable operators are Section 5D, and the spectral theorem for symmetric matrices is Section 7B.

From eigenvectors to a qubit

The rotation shows why this subject wants complex numbers. Over the real numbers a matrix may have no eigenvalue at all. Over the complex numbers every square matrix has one. Quantum mechanics takes that step from the start: its vectors have complex components.

The simplest case is the qubit of section 11. Its state is a vector with two complex components. Physicists write such a vector as a ket, and the two ways of writing translate term by term.

A quantity that can be measured is represented by a matrix as well, one that equals its own conjugate transpose. Such a matrix is called self-adjoint, or Hermitian. Its eigenvalues are real numbers, and they are the values that a measurement can give. Its eigenvectors are the states in which the result is certain.

Take the matrix Z, which tells |0⟩ from |1⟩. Here is its eigenvector calculation in both ways of writing.

With columns

With kets

Both say the same: the eigenvalues of Z are 1 and −1, the two values that a measurement of Z can give, and its eigenvectors are |0⟩ and |1⟩.

Coordinates depend on the basis you measure with. Choose two other basis vectors, and the same map gets a different matrix. Choose them along the eigenvectors of section 7, and the matrix becomes diagonal. Drag the red and blue arrows to choose the basis.

Try this

  2. Read the coordinates. Follow the red and blue walk to Ab1 = (2, 1): one step along b1, then one along b2. Its coordinates in the new grid are (1, 1), the first column of the new matrix. Ab2 = (3, 3) is three steps along b2: the second column is (0, 3).

What you just saw

If b1 and b2 form a basis, every vector v can be written in exactly one way as v = c1b1 + c2b2. The numbers c1 and c2 are the coordinates of v in that basis. Put the basis vectors into the columns of P, and this reads v = Pc, so the coordinates are c = P−1v.

Definition: the matrix of a map in a basis

The matrix of A in the basis b1, b2 is the matrix whose kth column holds the coordinates of Abk in that basis.

This is the rule of section 1 again: a column tells where a basis vector goes. Only the basis has changed. To compute the whole matrix at once, follow a vector through: its coordinates c give the vector Pc, the map sends it to APc, and P−1 turns that back into coordinates. So the new matrix is the product of three maps, as in section 5:

P−1AP

For it to exist, P must be invertible. By section 6 that happens exactly when nothing but 0 is crushed by P, which is to say when b1 and b2 do not lie on one line.

One new matrix, three ways

Take the opening picture: A has the rows (2, 1) and (1, 2), and the basis is b1 = (1, 0), b2 = (1, 1).

Column by column, as the walks in the picture do

Ab1 = (2, 1) = 1 b1 + 1 b2, Ab2 = (3, 3) = 0 b1 + 3 b2

The coordinates (1, 1) and (0, 3) are the two columns of the new matrix.

As a product of three matrices

P−1AP = 1−101 2112 1101 = 1013

Here P−1 comes from the formula of section 5, since det P = 1.

As a rule for coordinates, the way Axler writes it

T(x, y) = (2x + y,  x + 2y): Tb1 = b1 + b2,  Tb2 = 3b2

Axler writes the result as ℳ(T, (b1, b2)), the matrix of T with respect to the basis b1, b2. All three ways give the same matrix.

Two kinds of change

A matrix can stand for a map, as in sections 1 to 7, or for a change of description, as P does here. The arithmetic is the same. Only the reading differs: a map moves vectors, while a change of basis leaves them where they are and renames them.

The eigenbasis

Now take the eigenvectors of section 7 as the basis: b1 = (1, −1) with the eigenvalue 1 and b2 = (1, 1) with the eigenvalue 3. Since Ab1 = 1 b1 and Ab2 = 3 b2, the columns are (1, 0) and (0, 3), and the product confirms it:

½ 1−111 2112 11−11 = 1003

A diagonal matrix is the simplest kind there is: it stretches each basis vector by its own factor and does nothing else. A map that has a diagonal matrix in some basis is called diagonalizable, and that happens exactly when it has a basis of eigenvectors. Read backwards, the product says A = PDP−1: rename into the eigenbasis, stretch, rename back.

Some things do not depend on the basis at all. The determinant of P−1AP is det A, because by section 5 the factors det P−1 and det P cancel. Here 1 · 3 = 3 = 2 · 2 − 1 · 1. The eigenvalues do not change either: they belong to the map, not to its description.

The idea to keep. A matrix describes a map in one basis. In another basis the same map has the matrix P−1AP, and in a basis of eigenvectors that matrix is diagonal.

When it fails

A rotation of the plane has no real eigenvectors, and a shear has only one line of them. Neither can be made diagonal with real numbers. Over the complex numbers the rotation can, which is one more reason why quantum mechanics works with complex vectors.

A qubit in another basis

A qubit has more than one natural basis. Besides |0⟩, |1⟩ there is |+⟩, |−⟩, the two eigenvectors of X from section 7. Measuring a qubit in the second basis asks a different question, and section 11 shows where it points: along the x axis of the Bloch sphere instead of the z axis.

The preset Mirror is this change of basis. Its map is X, the reflection in the line y = x, and its basis vectors are |+⟩ and |−⟩ written as columns. The matrix with these columns is H. Since H is its own inverse, the matrix of X in the new basis is HXH.

With kets

X|+⟩ = |+⟩, and X|−⟩ = −|−⟩

The coordinates (1, 0) and (0, −1) are the columns of the new matrix.

With columns

HXH = ½ 111−1 0110 111−1 = 100−1 = Z

Measured in the basis |+⟩, |−⟩, the gate X has the matrix of Z. On the Bloch sphere this is no surprise. H is the half turn that swaps the x axis with the z axis, so a half turn about x, which is X, looks like a half turn about z, which is Z, once the axes have been renamed. Try it in section 11: apply H, then Z, then H, and compare with X.

9Lengths, angles and projections

The inner product turns lengths and angles into arithmetic. Drop a perpendicular from v onto a line, and the signed length of its shadow is an inner product. Measurement in quantum mechanics is built on exactly this picture. Drag v, and turn the basis by its red arrow.

Try this

  1. Read the picture. The red and blue arrows b1 and b2 have length 1 and stand at a right angle: an orthonormal basis. The dark arrow is v. The red and blue bands are its shadows on the two lines, where the dotted perpendiculars from v meet them.
  2. Turn the basis. Drag the tip of b1, or press Turn the basis. b2 follows at a right angle. The shadows change, but they always add up to v, and |v|2 in the panel never changes.
  3. Make a shadow vanish. Press Orthogonal, or turn the basis until b1 is at a right angle to v. Then ⟨b1, v⟩ = 0, and all of v lies along b2.
  4. Watch the squares. The bar splits |v|2 into the squared lengths of the two shadows. This is Pythagoras: the shadows are the sides of a rectangle, and v is its diagonal.
  5. Make v a state. Press Length 1. Now the two squares add up to 1, and the bar is headed as probabilities. Measure |+⟩ puts v halfway between the two basis vectors: 50% and 50%.
  6. Measure in another basis. Press Basis |±⟩. The basis is turned by 45°, and v = (1, 0) splits 50 to 50 between its lines. Drag v onto b1: in this basis the outcome becomes certain.

Reading with Axler

What you just saw

Definition: inner product and norm in the plane

For u = (u1, u2) and v = (v1, v2), the inner product is ⟨u, v⟩ = u1v1 + u2v2, and the norm, or length, of v is |v| = √⟨v, v⟩. Two vectors are orthogonal when their inner product is 0.

In the picture, ⟨u, v⟩ = |u| |v| cos θ, where θ is the angle between the arrows. When u has length 1, this is the signed length of the shadow of v on the line of u: positive when the shadow points along u, negative when it points the other way, and 0 at a right angle.

The shadow itself is a vector, the orthogonal projection of v onto the line. For a unit vector b it is

Pv = ⟨b, v⟩ b

One projection, three ways

Take v = (2, 1) and the line at 45°, with b = (1, 1)/√2.

With the inner product

⟨b, v⟩ = (2 + 1)/√2 = 3/√2, Pv = (3/√2) (1, 1)/√2 = (1.5, 1.5)

With a matrix

P = bbT = ½ 1111 and ½ 1111 21 = 1.51.5

With bras and kets

P = |b⟩⟨b|, P|v⟩ = |b⟩ ⟨b|v⟩

The bra ⟨b| is b written as a row. A row times a column is the inner product, and a column times a row is the matrix of the projection. The three lines are the same calculation in three notations.

Projecting twice

Projecting a shadow again leaves it where it is, so P2 = P. Its range is the line and its null space the perpendicular line, as in section 6, and its eigenvalues are 1 and 0, as in section 7.

Section 1 had one

The preset Flatten of section 1, with the columns (1, 0) and (0, 0), is the projection onto the horizontal axis. Not every flattening map is a projection, though: a projection drops its perpendicular at a right angle.

Orthonormal bases

When b1, b2 is an orthonormal basis, the coordinates of a vector are simply its shadows:

v = ⟨b1, v⟩ b1 + ⟨b2, v⟩ b2

So in section 8 no inverse has to be computed. The rows of PT are b1 and b2, so PTv lists the two inner products, which are the coordinates. Hence P−1 = PT. Since the shadows are the sides of a rectangle with diagonal v, Pythagoras gives

|v|2 = ⟨b1, v⟩2 + ⟨b2, v⟩2

A matrix that sends an orthonormal basis to an orthonormal basis keeps every length and every angle. Over the real numbers it is a rotation or a reflection, and UTU = I. Over the complex numbers it is called unitary, and the gates of section 11 are of this kind. That is why they keep the length 1 of a state and turn the sphere without stretching it.

Complex vectors

For complex vectors the first factor is conjugated: ⟨u|v⟩ = u1v1 + u2v2, where the bar marks the complex conjugate. Then ⟨v|v⟩ = |v1|2 + |v2|2 is never negative, as a squared length must be. The bra ⟨u| is the conjugated row, and everything above holds with ⟨b|v⟩2 replaced by |⟨b|v⟩|2.

The idea to keep. An inner product with a unit vector is the length of a shadow. In an orthonormal basis the shadows are the coordinates, and their squares add up to the squared length.

Which slot is conjugated

Axler makes his inner product linear in the first slot and conjugates the second. Physicists conjugate the first, so that ⟨u| can stand alone as a row. The two conventions differ only by a complex conjugate.

A measurement is a projection

Measuring a qubit in an orthonormal basis |b1⟩, |b2⟩ gives one of two outcomes. Outcome k comes with probability |⟨bk|ψ⟩|2, the squared length of the shadow of the state, and afterwards the state is |bk⟩. The probabilities add up to 1 by Pythagoras, because the state has length 1. This is the Born rule, and the bar in the panel shows it whenever v has length 1.

Take |ψ⟩ = |0⟩ and measure in the basis |+⟩, |−⟩, as in the preset Basis |±⟩.

With kets

⟨+|0⟩ = 1/√2, ⟨−|0⟩ = 1/√2, probabilities ½ and ½

With rows and columns

1/√2 11 10 = 1/√2

The ket |+⟩ is the vector b = (1, 1)/√2 of the example above, so the projection |+⟩⟨+| is the matrix P computed there. Measuring along |0⟩, |1⟩ asks about the z axis of the Bloch sphere, and measuring along |+⟩, |−⟩ asks about the x axis. In the plane of this section the two bases are 45° apart. On the sphere of section 11 they are 90° apart, because, as section 10 showed, every angle on the sphere is doubled.

10From two complex numbers to a point

A qubit state |ψ⟩ = α|0⟩ + β|1⟩ has two complex amplitudes, four real numbers in all, yet section 11 draws it as a point on a sphere, which needs only two. This section shows where the other two go. Drag the tips of the arrows α and β, and turn them.

Try this

  1. Read the picture. The red arrow is α, the amplitude of |0⟩, drawn in its own complex plane. The blue arrow is β, the amplitude of |1⟩. The sphere shows the point that the two make together.
  2. Turn both. Press Turn both. Both arrows go once round together, and the point on the sphere does not move. Multiplying the whole state by a phase changes nothing that can be measured.
  3. Turn only one. Press Turn β only. Now the angle φ between the arrows changes, and the point goes once round the sphere on the dotted circle, at a fixed height.
  4. Change the lengths. Drag the tip of α outwards. The arrow β gets shorter at the same time, because |α|2 + |β|2 must stay 1, and the point climbs towards |0⟩. The bar shows the two probabilities.
  5. Count the numbers. Four real numbers went in. The lengths are tied, so one is lost, and the overall phase cannot be seen, so another is lost. Two are left: θ and φ, the two angles of a point on a sphere.
  6. Many arrows, one state. Press |+i⟩. Drag α a quarter turn round, to point straight up: the angle between the arrows is gone, and the point moves to |+⟩. Now drag β a quarter turn as well, to point left. The point is back at |+i⟩, although both arrows have changed.

Reading with Axler

What you just saw

A complex number z = x + iy is a point of a plane, so it can be drawn as an arrow. Its length is |z| = √(x2 + y2) and its direction is an angle, its phase. Multiplying by a complex number of length 1, eiγ = cos γ + i sin γ, turns the arrow by γ and does not change its length.

Multiplying by i, two ways

Take z = 1 + i and multiply by i, using i2 = −1.

As complex numbers

i(1 + i) = i + i2 = −1 + i

As a matrix acting on the plane

0−110 11 = −11

Multiplying by i is the quarter turn of section 1. In general, multiplying by a + ib is the matrix with the rows (a, −b) and (b, a): a turn and a stretch in the sense of section 2, with the same stretch in every direction. This is also why the rotation of section 7 had the eigenvalues i and −i: over the complex numbers, turning is just multiplying.

Four numbers become two

Two facts about qubit states

The amplitudes satisfy |α|2 + |β|2 = 1, because the two probabilities add up to 1. And |ψ⟩ and eiγ|ψ⟩ describe the same physical state for every angle γ.

Use the second fact to turn both arrows until α points along the real axis. Then α is a number between 0 and 1, and the first fact fixes the length of β. Write the two lengths as the cosine and the sine of one angle, and the phase of β as φ:

|ψ⟩ = cos(θ/2) |0⟩ + eiφ sin(θ/2) |1⟩

This is the formula of section 11, and now each part has a picture. The lengths |α| and |β| are cos(θ/2) and sin(θ/2), and φ is the angle from the arrow α to the arrow β. The point on the sphere is

(x, y, z) = (sin θ cos φ,  sin θ sin φ,  cos θ)

The height is z = cos θ = |α|2 − |β|2, the difference of the two probabilities.

Why the angle is halved

The two lengths make the point (cos θ/2, sin θ/2) on a quarter circle, so θ/2 runs from 0 to 90°. The sphere doubles it, so that |0⟩ and |1⟩, at right angles as vectors, end up at opposite poles. The panel shows both angles.

One state, three ways

Take the state |+i⟩ and then multiply it by i.

With kets

|+i⟩ = (|0⟩ + i|1⟩)/√2, i|+i⟩ = (i|0⟩ − |1⟩)/√2

With columns

1/√2 1i and 1/√2 i−1

With lengths and phases

|α| = |β| = 1/√2, phases 0° and 90°, or 90° and 180°

The columns look different and the phases are different, but the lengths and the angle from α to β, 90°, are the same. So both give θ = 90° and φ = 90°, the point (0, 1, 0) on the sphere. Step 6 above shows it in the simulator.

The idea to keep. The lengths of α and β give the height of the point, and the angle between them gives how far round it is. Turning both arrows together changes nothing, which is why two complex numbers make only a point on a sphere.

11A gate turns the Bloch sphere

The state of a qubit is a vector of length 1 with two complex components, written |ψ⟩ = α|0⟩ + β|1⟩. Up to an overall phase, every such state is one point on a sphere. A gate is a 2 × 2 matrix that acts on the vector just as in section 1, and on the sphere every gate is a turn about some axis. Choose a gate, drag the state, and watch where it goes.

Try this

  1. Read the picture. The sphere opens on |ψ⟩ = |0⟩, the north pole, with the gate H. The gray arrow is the state before the gate. The dark arrow is U|ψ⟩, the state after it. H carries |0⟩ to |+⟩. The panel lists the two amplitudes of each state.
  2. Watch the turn. Press Play. The state travels along the green arc. The whole sphere makes half a turn about the green axis, which for H lies halfway between x and z. Drag anywhere on the picture, or move the View slider, to look from another side.
  3. Columns again. The matrix has two columns, U|0⟩ and U|1⟩. As in section 1, they say where the two basis states land, and the red and blue rings mark those places on the sphere. The two rings always sit at opposite ends of a diameter.
  4. Try the gates. Press X, Y and Z: half a turn about each of the three axes. S is a quarter turn about z, and T an eighth. Drag the state to other places, or use the six buttons under State, and try each gate again.
  5. Apply and go on. Press Apply gate. The state travels to its new place and stays there, ready for the next gate. Apply H, then choose T and apply it, then H again. The gray trail keeps the path. Reset returns to |0⟩.
  6. Find a state that does not move. Drag the state onto the green axis. The gate leaves it where it is. It is an eigenstate of the gate, and a gate that turns the sphere has two of them, one at each end of its axis.
  7. Make your own gate. Drag the green handle to move the axis, and set the angle with the Turn by slider. Every 2 × 2 unitary matrix is one of these turns, up to an overall phase.

Angles are doubled

|0⟩ and |1⟩ are at right angles as vectors, yet they sit at opposite poles of the sphere. Every angle on the sphere is twice the angle between the vectors. That is why the formulas below carry θ/2. Section 10 shows the two angles side by side.

An overall phase cannot be seen

Press Y with the state at |0⟩. The panel shows the amplitudes 0 and i, so U|ψ⟩ = i |1⟩, and the sphere shows the south pole. The factor i multiplies the whole state. No measurement can detect it, and the sphere leaves it out. In section 10 you can turn such a phase by hand.

What you just saw

A state α|0⟩ + β|1⟩ has two complex amplitudes with |α|2 + |β|2 = 1. Once the overall phase is set aside, two angles are enough to name it:

|ψ⟩ = cos(θ/2) |0⟩ + eiφ sin(θ/2) |1⟩

The angle θ is measured down from the north pole and φ around the vertical axis. The height z of the point gives the probabilities: a measurement gives 0 with probability cos2(θ/2), which is (1 + z)/2. The bar in the panel shows it for the dark arrow.

A gate is a unitary matrix U. It keeps the length of every vector, so the probabilities still add up to 1. Its columns are U|0⟩ and U|1⟩, and it acts on a state by the recipe of section 1:

U|ψ⟩ = α U|0⟩ + β U|1⟩

On the sphere this is a turn by some angle ω about an axis with direction (nx, ny, nz), counterclockwise as seen from the tip of the axis. Up to an overall phase, the matrix of that turn is

U = cos(ω/2) I − i sin(ω/2) (nxX + nyY + nzZ)

where X, Y and Z are the matrices of the three gates of step 4. This is the matrix the simulator shows whenever you set the axis or the angle yourself. The six named gates are shown in their customary form, which can differ from the formula by a phase: for half a turn about x the formula gives −iX.

The idea to keep. A qubit state is a point on a sphere, and a gate is a turn of the sphere. The matrix does to the vector (α, β) exactly what the turn does to the point.

Why a turn has two fixed points

When a sphere turns, the two ends of its axis stay where they are. As vectors they are eigenvectors of U, in the sense of section 7: the gate only multiplies each of them by a phase. For Z they are |0⟩ and |1⟩, for X they are |+⟩ and |−⟩, and for Y they are |+i⟩ and |−i⟩. Eigenvectors of a unitary matrix that belong to different eigenvalues are orthogonal, and orthogonal states are opposite points of the sphere.

Compare this with section 2. There every real matrix was a turn, a stretch and another turn. A unitary matrix is the case in which both stretch factors are 1. It changes no lengths and no angles between vectors, so the sphere moves as a rigid body: it turns.

About this chapter. These simulators are a companion to an algebra-first course. A text such as Axler’s Linear Algebra Done Right develops linear maps through definitions and proofs. Physicists and engineers usually also need to see what a map does to space, and a picture that moves under your hand is easier to remember than a formula. Each section is meant to be opened next to the matching pages of the book. The last section carries the same ideas to a first example from quantum mechanics, the state of one qubit.
